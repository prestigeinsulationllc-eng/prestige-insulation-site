Manufactured & Mobile Home Insulation: R-Values and Retrofit Tips

Manufactured homes aren't built to local residential energy code — they're built to the federal HUD Code, which uses its own thermal zone map and R-value standards. Here's how that works, and what to know if you're retrofitting an older unit.

HUD's three thermal zones

Unlike the IECC's climate zones used for site-built homes, HUD Code manufactured housing (24 CFR Part 3280) is designed to one of three thermal zones based on latitude:

HUD ZoneGeneral RegionDescription
Zone ISouthern U.S. — Florida, Gulf Coast, Southern California, etc.Warmest, lowest insulation requirement
Zone IIMid-latitude states — much of the central and mid-Atlantic U.S.Moderate
Zone IIINorthern U.S. — northern tier states, mountain regionsColdest, highest insulation requirement

Typical factory-installed R-values by zone

AssemblyZone IZone IIZone III
Ceiling/roofR-14R-19R-22 to R-33
WallsR-11R-11R-14 to R-19
Floor (belly)R-11R-14R-22

These are the standards the home was originally built to at the factory. If you're insulating a manufactured home yourself — for a repair, an addition, or a retrofit — match or exceed the value for your zone rather than assuming a one-size answer.

Retrofit: skirting and underbelly insulation

The most common insulation project on an existing manufactured home isn't the walls or roof — it's the underside. A few things worth knowing:

  • Skirting alone isn't insulation. Skirting blocks wind and pests around the home's perimeter, but it doesn't add meaningful R-value on its own. Insulated skirting panels or adding batt insulation to the belly above the skirting is what actually improves floor performance.
  • The belly board matters. The factory-installed underbelly is a fabric or board barrier holding floor insulation in place and keeping out moisture and pests. If it's torn or sagging, floor insulation underneath is often wet, compressed, or missing — that's usually the real cause of cold floors, not an underlying lack of R-value.
  • Batt width often differs from stick-built framing. Manufactured home wall and floor framing can use narrower or non-standard spacing than a site-built house — confirm actual cavity width before ordering rather than assuming standard 16" o.c. batt width.

Product notes for manufactured home jobs

Unfaced or kraft-faced fiberglass batts are the standard product for both new manufactured home construction and retrofit work, sized to the specific cavity being filled. For belly/floor retrofits where the space is being accessed from underneath, easier-to-handle faced batts or rolls are typically preferred over rigid materials.
